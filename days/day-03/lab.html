Calibration lab

Part A fits the thermal model to synthetic readings in the browser by hand and shows the sum of squared errors and the three ratios that the data determine. Part B matches symptoms with faults of the data. Part C computes one step of a Kalman filter by hand. Parts D, E and F repeat three methods of the lecture step by step, with sliders for their numbers: least squares by Gauss-Newton steps, the Kalman filter over eight readings and the Hampel test against the mean test. In these parts every intermediate value is rounded, and the next step continues with the rounded value, as in a calculation by hand.

Part A: The calibration bench

The dots are noisy temperature measurements of the pack over ninety minutes. Fit the model by hand with the four parameter sliders and watch the sum of squared errors, the sum of the squared differences between the dots and the curve. The four parameters are the internal resistance R0, the heat capacity m cp, the cooling conductance hA0 without the fan and the additional conductance hA1 at full fan. The noise alone leaves a sum of about 65, so no setting can go much lower. Then press Scale all four by 1.5: The curve does not move. Switch the experiment to fan never on and try to find the value of hA1.

The sliders move along the four parameters. The readout of the three ratios shows what the data actually determine: Equal ratios give an identical curve, whatever the four values are.

Part C: One step of a Kalman filter by hand

The model predicts 40.0 C with a variance of 0.5. The sensor reads 41.5 C with a noise variance of 0.25. The Kalman gain is the prior variance divided by the sum of the prior and the noise variances.

Continue in Colab, section 7: the Kalman filter on the pack over ninety minutes.

Part D: Calibrating a time constant, round by round

Least squares with one parameter, solved by Gauss-Newton steps as in the lecture. Four readings follow the heating of the pack at 40 A with the fan off, and the model is \(\hat{y}(t; \theta)\)​\({} = 36 + 31.73\,(1 - e^{-t/\theta})\), whose only parameter is the time constant \(\theta\) in seconds. Each round computes the model values and the residuals, the change of the model values when \(\theta\) grows by \(h = 100\) s, and the step. As in the optimiser of SciPy, a trust region limits every step: It may change \(\theta\) by at most half of its value. The sliders set the true time constant behind the readings, the noise added to them and the starting guess of the fit.

Continue in Colab, section 3: least squares with all four parameters of the pack.

Part E: The Kalman filter, reading by reading

The scalar filter of the lecture on eight readings of a pack that warms by 0.05 °C per step, from 41.00 to 41.35 °C. The belief starts at 40.00 °C with the variance \(P_0 = 1\), a very uncertain guess. Each step predicts with the model, a rise of 0.05 °C, and corrects with the reading. The first reading has no prediction before it. The sliders set the process noise variance \(Q\) and the standard deviation of the sensor noise that the filter assumes, \(R = \sigma^2\). The gain is rounded to four decimals and the belief to three.

Continue in Colab, section 7: the same filter on 541 readings of the calibration experiment.

Part F: The Hampel test against the mean test

The worked example of the lecture: seven readings with the value under test in the middle. The Hampel test compares its distance from the median with a limit built from the median absolute deviation (MAD), and the classical test compares its distance from the mean with a limit built from the standard deviation. The sliders set the value under test, a second value of the window and the factor of both limits, 3 in the lecture.

Continue in Colab, section 2: the Hampel filter on two hours of telemetry with twelve spikes.
